# Forcing questions

A diagnosis of whether a product is wanted, by whom, instead of what, and what the smallest paid version is. Use before market research or a plan. Ideas adapted from the YC office-hours method (gstack, MIT); the wording is this pack's own.

## 1. Principles

- **Specific beats general.** "Small clinics" is a filter. A named person at a named clinic with a named problem is a customer.
- **Interest is not demand.** Likes, waitlists, "interesting" and investor enthusiasm cost nothing. Money, repeat use, a workflow built around the product and annoyance when it breaks are demand.
- **The customer's words beat the pitch.** If users describe the value differently from the website, the users are right; rewrite the website.
- **Watch, do not demo.** Sit behind a user doing a real task and say nothing. Guided demos teach nothing.
- **The real competitor is the status quo.** A spreadsheet, a WhatsApp group, a notebook, a part-time assistant. If people do nothing today, the pain may be small.
- **Narrow first.** The smallest version someone pays for this week beats the platform you could build in a year.

## 2. How to run it

- Landscape check first (two minutes): search how the problem is solved today and who sells to this buyer, so the questions can name real alternatives. Findings are leads until cited.
- One question at a time. Use the host's question tool; plain chat is fine when there is none.
- Push on an answer until it is specific, recent and about real people. Push at least once on every answer: the first one is the polished one.
- Take a position after each answer (strong, weak, missing evidence) and name what would change your mind.
- Acknowledge a strong answer in one sentence and ask a harder follow-up. Warmth comes at the end.
- After the first answer, check the framing: undefined terms ("seamless", "AI platform"), hidden assumptions ("we need to raise money first"), real versus hypothetical evidence ("I think people would" versus "three owners did this last week"). Restate the product in plain words and ask if that is right; this takes a minute.
- Stage routing:

  | Stage | Ask |
  |---|---|
  | Idea, nothing built | 1, 2, 3 |
  | Prototype or early users | 2, 4, 5 |
  | Paying customers | 4, 5, 6 |
  | Infrastructure or internal tool | 2, 4 |

- Internal project inside a company: ask question 4 as "what is the smallest demo that gets the sponsor to say yes" and question 6 as "does this survive a reorg or the loss of your champion".

## 3. The six questions

### 1. Demand reality

Ask: what is the strongest evidence that someone wants this, someone who would be upset if it disappeared tomorrow?

Push until: a person paying, using it weekly, building their routine around it, or scrambling when it broke.

Red flags: "people say it is interesting", "500 on the waitlist", "investors like the space", "my friends love it".

Example push: "Loving an idea is free. Has anyone paid, asked when it ships, or been annoyed when the prototype broke? Name them."

### 2. Status quo

Ask: how do people solve this today, even badly, and what does the workaround cost them in time, money or mistakes?

Push until: a named workflow, hours per week, rupees lost, tools stitched together, a person hired to do it.

Red flags: "there is nothing like it" (so either the pain is small or they use something you have not seen).

### 3. Desperate specificity

Ask: name the one person who needs this most. What is their job, what gets them praised or blamed, what keeps them up at night?

Push until: a real name or a real, reachable role, plus a consequence they face ("loses a month of bookings", "gets shouted at by the owner").

Red flags: category answers ("SMEs", "marketing teams", "everyone"). You cannot message a category.

Match the consequence to the domain: career for B2B tools, daily pain for consumer tools, a blocked weekend project for hobby tools.

### 4. Narrowest wedge

Ask: what is the smallest version of this that someone would pay real money for this week, not after the platform is built?

Push until: one workflow, one report, one automation, even a manual service delivered by the founders.

Red flags: "we need the full platform first", "if we cut it down it is not differentiated". That is attachment to the design, not to the customer.

Bonus push: what would value look like if the user did nothing: no login, no integration?

### 5. Observation and surprise

Ask: have you watched someone use it without helping? What did they do that surprised you?

Push until: a concrete surprise that contradicts the plan. If nothing surprised you, you were not watching or not listening.

Red flags: "we ran a survey", "demo calls went well", "as expected". The best surprise is people using it for something it was not built for: that may be the real product.

### 6. Future fit

Ask: if the world is meaningfully different in three years, does the product become more necessary or less?

Push until: a specific claim about how the customer's world changes and why that raises the product's value.

Red flags: "the market grows 20% a year", "AI keeps improving". Every competitor can say that. What is your thesis?

## 4. Pushback patterns

| Founder says | Weak response | Strong response |
|---|---|---|
| "An AI tool for small businesses" | "Great market, let us explore" | "Which task does which owner lose two hours a week on? Name the person." |
| "Everyone I spoke to loves it" | "Encouraging!" | "Has anyone paid, scheduled a second call or asked for access? Love is not demand." |
| "We need the whole platform first" | "What would a lite version be?" | "If nobody can get value from a smaller version, the value is not clear yet. What would one person pay for this week?" |
| "The market grows 20% a year" | "A good tailwind" | "Growth is not a thesis. Why does that growth make your product more essential than the one next to it?" |
| "We want onboarding to be seamless" | "What is it now?" | "Seamless is a feeling. Which step loses people, how many, and have you watched one of them?" |

## 5. Demand evidence ladder

Report the highest rung reached, with names and dates.

| Rung | Evidence |
|---|---|
| 0 | Opinion: "I think they would" |
| 1 | Compliments, likes, "interesting" |
| 2 | Waitlist or sign-up with no further action |
| 3 | Time spent: interviews, trials, repeated calls |
| 4 | Money offered: pre-order, deposit, letter of intent with a price |
| 5 | Money paid, once |
| 6 | Paid again, upgraded, referred, or complained loudly when it broke |

Rung 3 or lower: the next step is an experiment to climb one rung, not a build.

## 6. Failure patterns to name out loud

- Solution in search of a problem.
- Hypothetical users ("people like me").
- Interest mistaken for demand.
- Waiting for the perfect launch.
- Platform before wedge.
- Building for investors instead of customers.
- No access to the buyer (the user and the payer are different people and you have met neither).
- Competing on features with an incumbent instead of on a segment they ignore.

## 7. Read-out

```
Stage: ...   Highest demand rung: ... (evidence: names, dates)
Strongest answer: ...   Weakest answer: ...
Status quo and its cost: ...
Named first customer: ...
Narrowest paid version: ...
Position: build / narrow / test first / stop  (what would change it)
Assignment this week: <who, how many, what counts as success, by when>
```

## 8. Builder mode (hobby, learning, open source)

If the owner is not trying to build a company, drop the demand interrogation and ask generative questions: what would make you use this yourself every day, what is the most fun thing you could ship in a weekend, who would you show it to first, what is the cool version that is easy to build. If they start talking about customers, revenue or raising money, switch to the questions above and say so.

# Brand discovery

Goal: learn enough about the product and its people to make design choices that could not belong to anyone else. Fast when the owner is busy, deeper when the brand is new.

## 1. Read before asking

Look in the repo first: README, landing copy, pitch or business docs (`docs/`, `company/`), existing `DESIGN.md`, `brand/`, logo files, current CSS or theme, app store listing text, pricing page. Most answers are there. Ask only for what is missing.

## 2. The brief (fill what you can, mark the rest "assumed")

| Field | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Product | What is it, in one sentence a customer would use? |
| People | Who uses it, how skilled, how often, on what device, in what mood? |
| Job | What do they come to get done? What goes wrong today? |
| Use scene | Where and under what light do they use it (desk at noon, phone on a train at night, shop floor)? This decides light vs dark and density. |
| Category and peers | Who else is in the space? What do they all look like? What would be the cliché? |
| Memorable thing | After seeing the product once, what is the one thing someone should remember: a feeling, a visual, a claim, a posture? Every later decision serves it. |
| Adjectives | Three to five words for how it should feel, plus three it must never feel (anti-adjectives). Adjectives become visual cues. |
| Anti-references | Products, sites or looks the owner dislikes and why. |
| Constraints | Existing logo colours, platforms, languages and scripts, accessibility needs, performance budget, legal marks, brand equity to keep. |
| Surfaces | Which of: marketing site, app UI, docs, email, print, social, slides, packaging. Each may need a different register. |
| Voice | How would a person at the company say this out loud? Formal or casual, technical or plain? |
| Decision maker | Who signs off? One person or a committee? |

## 3. Fast track

When the owner wants speed or gives little: ask at most five questions in one message, recommended option first (product in a sentence, people and use scene, three adjectives, two anti-references, constraints). Or skip questions, state the assumptions as a short list, and go straight to a proposal. The owner reacts faster to a concrete proposal than to a questionnaire.

## 4. Turn adjectives into visual cues

| Adjective | Cues to try (not rules) |
|---|---|
| Trustworthy, calm | restrained palette, generous space, steady grid, humanist or neutral type, low-contrast motion |
| Precise, technical | tight grid, tabular figures, mono for data, cool neutrals, sharp or small radii |
| Warm, friendly | rounded shapes, warm neutrals, larger type, illustrations drawn by hand or photographed people, soft motion |
| Premium, quiet | one accent, deep neutrals, fine type with room, slow subtle motion, few elements |
| Playful | saturated colour, bold shapes, springy motion, expressive type, humour in microcopy |
| Urgent, energetic | high contrast, dense, strong type, fast motion, signal colours used sparingly |
| Editorial, literary | type-led layout, strong hierarchy, pull quotes, asymmetry, long measure for reading |
| Local, handmade | texture, real photography, imperfect shapes, regional colour and script |

Anti-adjectives matter as much: "never corporate" removes a set of blue-grey SaaS cues.

## 5. Word map

Write the product name, what it does, the adjectives and the promise in the centre of a page. Around them list nouns, metaphors, materials, places, objects and opposites. Circle the intersections. These supply motifs, textures and vocabulary for the visual world. Draw colour and imagery from the subject's own world (its materials, tools, places, typography), not from the category's stereotype.

## 6. Category clichés

List what everyone in the category does (fintech: navy, upward arrows, shields; wellness: sage green and soft serif; developer tools: near-black with neon accent; coffee: beans and steam). Treat them as off-limits unless you can give them a fresh form. Then decide what the product shares with the category on purpose (people need to recognise what it is) and where it departs (people remember it).

## 7. Output

A one-page brief: the table above filled, assumptions marked, the memorable-thing sentence, the use-scene sentence, adjectives and anti-adjectives, and the list of category clichés to avoid. Save it as the "Overview" part of `DESIGN.md`, not as a separate document.

## 8. Checks before leaving discovery

1. The memorable-thing sentence is specific enough that a competitor could not claim it.
2. The use scene decides light or dark.
3. There are anti-adjectives, not only adjectives.
4. Constraints include accessibility and language needs.
5. Nothing in the brief is invented; assumptions are labelled.
